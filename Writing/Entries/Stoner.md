It was getting late and sleep was slowly tightening its grip on me. It may be that I am becoming old or simply that I'm becoming a creature of habit. I have this ritual where I have to read for a couple of minutes before going to bed. Doing so signals my brain to shut off operations for the day, during this time my only task is to relax and surrender to Morpheus. 

Before I had this ritual in place, I watched YouTube, frying my dopamine receptors until I was anesthetized.

My time with Stoner by John Williams was coming to a close. I had 2 chapters left. My plan was to read one that night, and leave the last for tomorrow morning. I finished the second to last chapter, placed the coffee bag label I was using as a bookmark inside and closed the book. 

The brain ignored the signal, it was hard to succumb to sleep. I thought about how we never notice when we fall asleep. It just happens reducing hours to a mere instant to conclude with the beginning of a new day. A second though followed this, "funny how almost every living thing sleeps". Then a third idea, I thought about William Stoner, his presence kept nagging at me. Why did I felt so melancholic about him? I resumed my reading and finished the book. I knew how it would end. The first chapter of the book tells you how the life of Stoner ends. But still I felt a tightness in my chest, similar to how it feels like to say goodbye to a lifelong friend you haven't seen in a while, not knowing when I will see him again. *maybe could improve the flow of the last sentence*

This was clearly result of John William's simple, yet evocative prose. Following Stoner's life, from a humble farmer, becoming a father, obtaining his doctorate in literature to his eventual death is such an intimate experience that it becomes inevitable to create an emotional attachment to the character. 

Reading his life through the pages in this book I have juxtaposed my own life with his. I got a chance to reach some conclusions which I want to share in this chronicle.

# I envy Stoner, for his passion found him.

Stoner did not find his passion, it found him. I looked back at my life, and all I saw was what seemed like an eternal search. Reading Stoner's life I saw a man that lived for literature, everything he did was aimed at the well-being of his students and the furthering of his studies. It is never mentioned in the book, but I doubt that he ever thought about being anything else but a teacher.

Stoner entered college to study Agriculture with the aim to help his father with the family farm. Due to the curriculum, he had to take a perfunctory class in English.

William struggled during the first weeks of this class, not getting satisfactory grades in his exams. He read and reread the books assigned to him, but not going further than thinking words were just words on paper, finding no use for them. 

During one lecture, Sloane, his teacher then and mentor after, was reading a passage by Shakespeare and proceeds to ask his class what it meant.

```
“What does the sonnet mean?” he asked abruptly, and paused, his eyes
searching the room with a grim and almost pleased hopelessness.

Sloane’s eyes came back to William Stoner, and he said dryly, “Mr.
Shakespeare speaks to you across three hundred years, Mr. Stoner; do you
hear him?”
```

When Sloane asked Stoner directly, something inside of him was shaken, he became aware of his existence, he felt his cloths touching his skin, his breath coming and going from his body, he noticed how the light shone on and bounced off his classmates. Bewitched by the sonnet, he struggled to provide an answer but from that day on, literature started to nourish his life. 

```
He became conscious of himself in a way that he had not done before.
Sometimes he looked at himself in a mirror, at the long face with its thatch
of dry brown hair, and touched his sharp cheekbones; he saw the thin wrists
that protruded inches out of his coat sleeves; and he wondered if he
appeared as ludicrous to others as he did to himself.
```

A couple of years later after graduating from his English Literature undergraduate program, World War I breaks out. Stoner's friends were ready to answer the call to arms. But William was not sure about enlisting. He pays Sloane a visit, to reach clarity. Perhaps, due to his years of experience or maybe seeing some part of him in Stoner, Sloane observed without hesitation:

```
“It’s love, Mr. Stoner,” Sloane said cheerfully. “You are in love. It’s as
simple as that.”
```

Sloane pushed him to become a teacher and this single decision had set flight to Stoner's life.

### Not being sure about the future but still committing
He saw the future in the institution to which he had committed himself and which he so imperfectly understood; he conceived himself changing in that future, but he saw the future itself as the instrument of change rather than its object.

In the summer of 1937 he felt a renewal of the old passion for study and
learning; and with the curious and disembodied vigor of the scholar that is the condition of neither youth nor age, he returned to the only life that had not betrayed him. He discovered that he had not gone far from that life even in his despair.


As he worked on the room, and as it began slowly to take a shape, he
realized that for many years, unknown to himself, he had had an image
locked somewhere within him like a shamed secret, an image that was
ostensibly of a place but which was actually of himself. So it was himself that he was attempting to define as he worked on his study. As he sanded the old boards for his bookcases, and saw the surface roughness disappear, the gray weathering flake away to the essential wood and finally to a rich purity of grain and texture—as he repaired his furniture and arranged it in the room, it was himself that he was slowly shaping, it was himself that he was putting into a kind of order, it was himself that he was making possible.

### Immersed in his work
Within a few moments he was immersed in his work. The evening
before, he had caught up with the routine of his classwork; papers had been
graded and lectures prepared for the whole week that was to follow. He sawthe evening before him, and several evenings more, in which he would be
free to work on his book. What he wanted to do in this new book was not
yet precisely clear to him; in general, he wished to extend himself beyond
his first study, in both time and scope. He wanted to work in the period of
the English Renaissance and to extend his study of classical and medieval

and it was that stage which gave him the most pleasure—the selection
among alternative approaches, the rejection of certain strategies, the
mysteries and uncertainties that lay in unexplored possibilities, the
consequences of choice ....The possibilities he could see so exhilarated him

kind of frustrated joy spoke to his daughter, who looked up from her book
and answered him.

### Finding his book after a while:
It hardly mattered to him that the book was forgotten and that it served no use; and the question of its worth at any time seemed almost trivial. He did not have the illusion that he would find himself there, in that fading print; and yet, he knew, a small part of him that he could not deny was there, and would be there.
He opened the book; and as he did so it became not his own. He let his
fingers riffle through the pages and felt a tingling, as if those pages were alive. The tingling came through his fingers and coursed through his flesh and bone; he was minutely aware of it, and he waited until it contained him, until the old excitement that was like terror fixed him where he lay. The sunlight, passing his window, shone upon the page, and he could not see what was written there.


### Finding happiness in his work:
The years immediately following the end of the Second World War were the
best years of his teaching; and they were in some ways the happiest years of his life. Veterans of that war descended upon the campus and transformed it, bringing to it a quality of life it had not had before, an intensity and turbulence that amounted to a transformation. He worked harder than he had ever worked; the students, strange in their maturity, were intensely serious and contemptuous of triviality. Innocent of fashion or custom, they came to their studies as Stoner had dreamed that a student might—as if those studies were life itself and not specific means to specific ends. He knew that never, after these few years, would teaching be quite the same; and he committed himself to a happy state of exhaustion which he hoped might never end. He seldom thought of the past or the future, or of the disappointments and joys of either; he concentrated all the energies of which he was capable upon the moment of his work and hoped that he was at last defined by what he did.

### What is love
In his extreme youth Stoner had thought of love as an absolute state of
being to which, if one were lucky, one might find access; in his maturity he
had decided it was the heaven of a false religion, toward which one ought to
gaze with an amused disbelief, a gently familiar contempt, and an
embarrassed nostalgia. Now in his middle age he began to know that it was
neither a state of grace nor an illusion; he saw it as a human act of
becoming, a condition that was invented and modified moment by moment
and day by day, by the will and the intelligence and the heart.
